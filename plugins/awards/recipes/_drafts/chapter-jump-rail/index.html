The Orrin Line

Seventy-four kilometres from Saltby harbour to the cairn on Orrin Moor, walked in six stages. Each stage is a chapter of this guide. The list of stages follows you as you read, and takes you to any of them.

Stage 1 From the harbour gate to the first stile

The line starts at the green gate where the fish quay meets the road, under the tide clock that has run eleven minutes slow for as long as anyone in Saltby remembers. Turn your back on the net lofts and follow the sea wall east.

For the first hour the path keeps to the top of the wall, with the harbour mud on one side and allotments on the other. The waymarks here are small brass discs set into the coping stones; they are easy to miss in rain, so count the groynes instead. The seventh groyne is where the wall ends and the shingle begins.

Past the coastguard cottages the path climbs a few metres onto the old railway bed and runs straight for three kilometres. It is the easiest walking of the whole line, which is why most people go too fast and arrive at the first stile with tired feet and two hours of tide still to wait.

Continue to stage 2, the Salt Flats

Stage 2 Across the salt flats at low water

The flats are the reason the line exists. A causeway of timber posts crosses four kilometres of sand and samphire to the far shore, and it is dry for about five hours either side of low water. The tide table at the stile is kept by the Trust and is the only one to trust.

Walk from post to post and never cut a corner, however firm the sand looks. The channels move every winter, and the posts are moved with them; a post with a white band marks a channel you will wade, usually to the knee, sometimes higher after rain on the moor.

Halfway across there is a refuge tower with a ladder and a bench. If the water turns before you reach it, climb, sit and wait: the flats empty again in a few hours, and the tower has seen more patient walkers than drowned ones.

Continue to stage 3, Kestrel Ridge

Stage 3 Up Kestrel Ridge in a west wind

From the far shore the path turns inland and goes straight up the nose of the ridge. There is no gentle way onto Kestrel; the old drove road zigzags for the cattle, and the line follows it, so the climb is long rather than steep.

Once on the crest the wind arrives, almost always from the west and almost always stronger than the forecast. The ridge is broad and grassy, the path a worn green strip between heather, and the birds the ridge is named for hang over the slope on your left, facing into the wind and not moving at all.

The trig pillar at the top is the highest point of the first half. On a clear day you can see the causeway posts behind you and the dark edge of Lowmoor Wood ahead, and between them the whole of the morning you have just walked.

Continue to stage 4, Lowmoor Wood

Stage 4 Down through Lowmoor Wood

The wood begins at a deer gate and closes over the path within a hundred steps. These are old sessile oaks, short and crooked, grown out of coppice stools that were last cut when the iron works in the valley still needed charcoal.

Look for the charcoal platforms: flat circles of black earth the size of a small room, cut into the slope every few hundred metres. Nothing grows well on them even now. The forester's hut at the halfway clearing is unlocked, has a stove and a visitors' book, and asks only that you leave the kindling box full.

The path leaves the wood by a footbridge over the Callow, the river you will follow for the whole of the next stage. Fill your bottle above the bridge, not below it: the farm upstream is careful, the one downstream less so.

Continue to stage 5, the Weirs

Stage 5 Along the weirs to Callow Bridge

Five weirs step the Callow up its valley, each built for a mill that has long since become a house, a barn or a heap of dressed stone in the nettles. The path keeps to the left bank and climbs a little at every weir, which is why the stage feels flat and is not.

The third weir has a fish pass, a staircase of pools cut beside the sill, and in October the salmon can be watched from the footbridge throwing themselves at it. Stand still and they come close; they are not interested in you at all.

Callow Bridge is a hamlet of a dozen houses, a chapel turned into a bunkhouse and an inn that serves food until nine. Most walkers stop here for the night, because the last stage is the hardest and is best started early.

Continue to stage 6, Orrin Cairn

Stage 6 The last climb to Orrin Cairn

Above Callow Bridge the fields give out, then the walls, then the trees, and for the last eight kilometres there is only the moor: bog cotton, peat hags and a line of stone posts that the Trust rebuilds every spring after the frost has thrown them down.

The path is wet in all seasons. Keep to the posts in mist, and if the mist is thick, count them; there are one hundred and twelve between the last wall and the cairn, and a laminated card in the bunkhouse lists the bearing from each to the next.

Orrin Cairn is a heap of stones taller than a person, added to by everyone who finishes the line. Find a stone on the way up and carry it. From the top, on the rare clear day, you can see the sea at Saltby and the whole of the six days laid out between.

Before you set out

Carry the tide table for stage 2 and a map for stage 6, whatever your phone says. The Trust's bunkhouses at the Salt Flats stile and at Callow Bridge take bookings from March to October.
