## What the water leaves

Coast / Mara Vale / 18 September 2026 / 6 min read / Extended excerpt

### The first line

The morning begins without a clear edge. Cloud sits low enough to soften the far shore, and the water borrows its colour until the two almost meet. Nearer our feet, everything is specific: a pale stone with a dark seam, a curl of olive weed, the sharp step where one layer of slate ends and another begins. We walk toward the detail because the distance offers so little to hold.

At the high mark, a thin line of fragments traces the previous reach of the water. It is not straight. It bends around stones, breaks at shallow channels, gathers again where the beach dips. From a distance the line looks like a boundary. Up close it is a collection of separate arrivals. A seed head rests beside a piece of softened wood. A length of weed has caught on a pebble no larger than a thumb.

### A temporary arrangement

We stop beside a pool held between flat rocks. Its surface is still, though the water beyond it moves in small folds. Looking down, we can see stones with a clarity that will disappear as soon as they dry. Grey deepens toward blue. A brown fleck becomes a small bright point. The wet surfaces do not reveal a different beach; they give the same beach a different emphasis.

There is a temptation to make the arrangement permanent in a photograph, to frame the pool as if it were composed for us. But a frame leaves out the wind against a sleeve, the unsteady place to stand, and the repeated sound of loose stones settling under a thin wash. The image is useful precisely because it is incomplete. It gives us something to return to, and something to remember beyond its edges.

A few steps farther on, slate rises in narrow shelves. Each shelf holds a different amount of water. Some are already matte; others catch a broad reflection of the sky. We trace one line with our eyes and lose it beneath another. Nothing here is as regular as it first appears. Repetition becomes interesting at the point where it changes.

### Keeping a modest record

In the notebook, we write what we can see before writing what we think it means. Rounded pebbles. Three shallow pools. Weed laid in a loose arc. A horizon that appears and disappears. The list is plain, but it keeps the observation open. Later, it may suggest a story. For now, it is enough to have looked carefully.

By the time we turn back, the light has shifted without becoming brighter. The water is closer to the first stone we noted, and the pool has joined a narrow channel. Our temporary arrangement is already being revised. We leave the pebbles where they are and carry back only the record: a few lines, two images, and the sense that an ordinary place can remain unfinished.
