## Brief
- **Subject:** The Museum of Extinct Colours, a fictional one-room museum in a former pharmacy on the Hoogstraat in Antwerp, which shows seven pigments that can no longer be bought, each beside the reason it ended: Egyptian blue (the recipe lost after Rome, found again in the nineteenth century), Tyrian purple (about 12,000 sea snails for 1.4 grams), lead-tin yellow (forgotten in the eighteenth century), Indian yellow (made from the urine of cows fed on mango leaves, gone by the early twentieth century), Scheele's green (copper arsenite, 1775, the poison in the wallpaper), mummy brown (ground Egyptian mummies; the last London supplier said in the 1960s it had run out) and uranium red (the glaze that stopped in 1943 when the uranium went to war). Synthetic: the museum, its address, its rooms, its staff, its prices and its opening hours are invented for this build. The pigment histories are real and every fact carries its confidence label in the copy.
- **Unique mechanism (what only this subject can prove):** a colour is a recipe and a supply chain, and when either breaks the colour goes extinct; each of the seven ended for a different reason (lost, used up, poisonous, unpleasant to make, requisitioned). The page can show a screen approximation of each colour beside the cause, and say honestly that a screen can only approximate a pigment it has never seen.
- **Audience and their real scene:** painters, illustrators, designers and people who visit small museums on a weekend; on a phone on a tram or on a laptop on a Sunday morning, reading for pleasure, with time to look.
- **Stakes and conversion (what a visitor should do, matched to the price of the decision):** a €12 timed viewing of the drawer room (six people, twenty minutes, Tuesday to Sunday). The action is "Book a viewing, €12": choose a day and a time slot in a short form that answers with a booking number; nothing is charged, and the form says so. In the first viewport and at the close.
- **Assets on hand:** none. Copy and the museum are synthetic and written for the build. Every colour is an sRGB approximation computed from published descriptions and labelled as such; the objects (the drawers, the vials, the labels) are drawn or rendered from code. No photography, no stock, no generated rasters, no museum's images.
- **Binding constraints:** Vite + vanilla JS, static hosting from any folder (relative base); WCAG 2.2 AA; three motion tiers; no third-party requests at runtime; `programs: awwwards` (Site of the Day as the target).
- **Routes:** single page + 404.
- **Sound:** none.
- **Visitor mode:** experience, read-led (an exhibition the visitor can scan: the seven colours reachable from the first screen, a visit booked at the end).
- **References the user likes:** none.
- **Calibration:** wins on Design and Creativity (seven colours that no longer exist, each in its own room, each with a reason it ended); most likely to lose on Usability and Content accuracy (colour as the content means contrast changes room by room; seven historical claims must hold). The usability walk checks first: contrast of every text on every pigment ground, a keyboard path to all seven colours from the first screen, and the booking form by keyboard.

## Direction contract
- **THESIS:** The page is a paint cross-section under the museum's microscope: a sliver of seven paint layers set in a resin block, the newest colour on top and the oldest at the bottom, and the visitor's scroll moves the microscope down through it, from mummy brown (run out in the 1960s) to Egyptian blue (the first pigment anyone made, around 2600 BCE), each layer filling the screen as the grains of a colour no one can buy, beside the reason it ended. Refused: the category rut (a museum page: a hero photograph of the building, opening hours in a bar, "What's on" cards in a grid, "Visit us"); the Awwwards rut (a dark page with a glowing accent, a paint-splash or fluid blob hero, particles floating for mood, sections that fade and rise, a cursor trail of colour); the template skeleton (wordmark left and links right, a label over a two-line headline at bottom left, a subline, a filled plus outline button pair over a full-bleed image).
- **WORLD:** Achromatic laboratory with chroma from the specimen only: a cool off-white ground like the light of a microscope's field and a near-black ink carry every word; the only colours on the page are the seven pigments themselves, as grains in their layers, and nothing else may borrow them for decoration. Three tokens (ground, ink, one muted rule colour) and the seven pigment approximations, which are data, labelled as approximations, never chrome. Light, because a microscope's field is light and a pigment is judged under daylight; one chapter goes dark because the infrared view is dark. Type contract: one display face with the precision of an engraved specimen label (high contrast or finely drawn, never a reflex face) for the colour names and the claim; one quiet text face for the labels and the histories, with old-style figures for dates; small capitals for the catalogue numbers. Material policy: the resin block, the glass slide and the paper museum label are the only objects; the grains are the one rendered material, each pigment with its own grain (Egyptian blue as angular blue glass, Tyrian purple fine and dense, lead-tin yellow small and rounded, Indian yellow translucent, Scheele's green fine and bright, mummy brown with fibre and resin, uranium red as glaze); no glow except the one infrared chapter, no gradients as decoration, no paint splashes. Imagery strategy: code — a procedural microscope field per pigment and a drawn resin block; no photography, no stock, no generated rasters.
- **STORY:** Chaptered journey; the medium retraces how a conservator reads paint: the block, the microscope, the layers in order of depth, then the infrared lamp. (1) The block — the cross-section sits in its resin block at low magnification, seven thin bands of colour · the claim, what the museum is, and the booking action · the microscope moves in to the top layer. (2) Used up and taken, 1964 · 1943 — mummy brown, then uranium red, fill the field · two labels: the last tube of mummies, the glaze the war took · the boundary into the next layer. (3) Unbearable and poisonous, c. 1910 · c. 1900 — Indian yellow, then Scheele's green · two labels: the mango-leaf cows, the arsenic in the wallpaper · the boundary. (4) Forgotten and too dear, c. 1750 · 1453 — lead-tin yellow, then Tyrian purple · two labels: the yellow the old masters used and the world forgot, the 12,000 snails for 1.4 grams · the boundary. (5) The interruption: the lights go out — the deepest layer, Egyptian blue, on a held stage; the visible light dims and only its grains glow, in infrared · the oldest made colour, how it was lost and how a camera finds it now · the lights come back. (6) The drawer room — the block again, all seven layers named · booking a viewing, the sources for every claim · the museum's colophon. One interruption (chapter 5); the close is the booking and a credits list; the 404 is an empty layer in the resin.
- **FIRST VIEWPORT:** Archetype: single object with inertia, adapted — the object is the resin block holding the cross-section, never a product turntable. At 1440 × 900: the block, a grey translucent rounded rectangle about 30 % of the width, stands on the right half of the screen, slightly turned, its polished face showing the cross-section as seven thin horizontal bands of colour, mummy brown on top and Egyptian blue at the bottom, with a hairline scale bar (1 mm) and catalogue number beside it. The left half: the claim at display scale on three lines, flush left on the 1/12 column, top at about 24 %; under it one line saying what the museum is and where; then the action "Book a viewing, €12" as a button in ink with the next free slot beside it. The museum's line across the top is the navigation: the name at the left, the seven colour names as links (each with a tiny chip of its colour), "Book" at the right. Nothing moves on load but the block, which turns about 8° and settles in 1.2 s. Phone: the block sits under the claim, centred, as large as the width allows; the claim on four lines; the action under it inside the first screen; the colour links collapse into a menu.
- **SIGNATURE:** The microscope descends through the paint. Physical metaphor: a conservator moving a microscope's stage down a paint cross-section. A fixed field behind the page renders the layer under the current scroll at 400× magnification as that pigment's own grains in its binder; as the visitor scrolls, the stage moves down the section, the irregular boundary between two paints crosses the screen, and a stage readout prints the depth in micrometres, the layer's colour name and the year it ended. Jobs: (1) it enacts the thesis — the visitor goes down through time, one lost colour after another, and sees each as material, not as a swatch; (2) wayfinding — the readout's depth and the colour links are the page's progress; (3) the chapter transitions — every layer boundary is a chapter boundary; (4) the interruption — the same field switches to the infrared view. Technique tier: canvas-first, one fullscreen GL field (lazy) behind a DOM document; every word, name, date and label is DOM, and each layer has a still. Touch: the same scroll. Keyboard: the seven colour links jump to their layers; the infrared view has a real toggle button. Reduced motion: the field cuts from layer to layer at the boundaries, no continuous travel, the block does not turn; the infrared view is a cut between two stills. Fallback without WebGL or on the low tier: each layer as a static procedural field drawn in CSS and SVG (turbulence and the layer's colour), the same readouts.
- **SCROLL MODEL:** Native + Lenis, with one sticky stage on an invisible rail for the infrared interruption (about 160 vh). The exhibition is a document a visitor scans and returns to, colour by colour, so the scroll stays native, the colour links jump, and find-in-page works; only the infrared moment needs a held frame.
- **LOAD & CLOSE:** No preloader and no gate: the first viewport paints from the DOM with the block and its seven bands drawn in CSS; the GL field starts behind the page after its first rendered frame (the real signal), with no shift. Repeat visits change nothing. The last screen is the drawer room: the booking form (day, time slot, number of people) beside the block with all seven layers named, then a credits list of the sources for every claim, and the museum's colophon. The 404 ships with the first release: an empty layer in the resin, one line, three routes back.

  - `[site:a24-raviklaassens]` — take: keep the page achromatic so the work is the only colour, and generate the object in code instead of loading it (the grain fields and the block are procedural). Refuse: the optical disc, the tilted row and the hand-drawn ring, its paper, ink and oxblood values, and the serif-caps, grotesque and spaced-label trio.

Refuse: the jars on a tilted ring over an emblem, the turning circular motto, the falling-fruit loader and its cream, oxblood and orange palette.
