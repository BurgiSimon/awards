## Brief
- **Subject:** C/S Petrel, a fictional cable repair ship based in Ponta Delgada (Azores, 37.74° N, 25.67° W) that mends broken internet cables on the floor of the North Atlantic, and the crew that runs her fault desk. Synthetic: the ship, her owner, her crew, her dimensions, her standby agreement and every name are invented for this build. The method (fault location from shore, grapnel recovery, the final splice and the bight laid back on the seabed) and the physical figures (light in fibre, ocean depths, cable diameters, fault counts) are real and labelled with their confidence in the copy.
- **Unique mechanism (what only this subject can prove):** the internet is a physical thing lying on the seabed, and when it breaks a ship drags a hook across the bottom of the ocean, blind, at four kilometres, and lifts it. The page measures the one thing every visitor carries: how long their own light takes to cross the sea (5 µs per kilometre of fibre, computed in the browser from their time zone to the nearest landing station), so the repair is about their packet, not an abstraction.
- **Audience and their real scene:** first, technologists who never think about the wet part of the network: on a laptop between builds, or on a phone in a queue, curious, ten minutes of attention. Second, network-operations engineers at cable owners who need a repair ship on standby: at a desk in a NOC with an alarm open, scanning for response time, coverage and how to open a ticket.
- **Stakes and conversion (what a visitor should do, matched to the price of the decision):** for cable owners a standby agreement is a six-figure annual decision, so the action is not a sale but a line to the fault desk: "Report a cable fault", a short ticket form (cable, last good repeater, first alarm in UTC, contact) that answers with a ticket number and the ship's reply window. In the first viewport and at the close. Nothing is sent; the form says so.
- **Assets on hand:** none. Copy, the ship, the crew lines and the agreement are synthetic and written for the build. The seabed profile, the cable and the grapnel are drawn or rendered from code; ocean depths along the route come from public bathymetry figures quoted as rounded values. No photography, no stock, no generated rasters.
- **Binding constraints:** Vite + vanilla JS, static hosting from any folder (relative base); WCAG 2.2 AA; three motion tiers; no third-party requests at runtime; `programs: awwwards` (Site of the Day as the target).
- **Routes:** single page + 404.
- **Sound:** none.
- **Visitor mode:** experience, with a persuade tail (the repair told as it happens; coverage, response time and the fault desk in the second half).
- **References the user likes:** none.
- **Calibration:** wins on Creativity and Content (a real operation at four kilometres, numbers with units on every screen, the visitor's own latency); most likely to lose on Usability (a long scrubbed operation, depth readouts that change under text, a dark ground at depth). The usability walk checks first: a rail out of the operation at every chapter, the fault-desk action by keyboard from the first viewport, and contrast of every readout at every depth.

## Direction contract
- **THESIS:** The page is the repair cable itself, paying out over the Petrel's bow sheave: the visitor's scroll is the cable engine, lowering the grapnel through 4,212 metres of North Atlantic to the broken internet, hooking it, lifting both ends to the deck and laying the mended cable back on the seabed, with the depth, the metres paid out and the line tension printed beside it the whole way. Refused: the category rut (a B2B service page: a hero metric row, a three-feature grid of "coverage, speed, expertise", a fleet photo, "Let's talk"); the Awwwards rut (a near-black page from the first pixel with a glowing accent, a particle or "marine snow" field, bioluminescent glow, sections that fade and rise, a cursor trail); the template skeleton (wordmark left and links right, a label over a two-line headline bottom left, a subline, a filled plus outline button pair over a full-bleed video of a ship at sea).
- **WORLD:** Theme by depth: three base tokens (ground, ink, accent) re-pointed per depth band, the grounds derived from how sunlight dies in seawater: surface haze at 0 m, the blue that survives at 200 m, a near-black blue below 1,000 m where no sunlight reaches, and the pale grey ooze of the abyssal plain at the seabed; the ink inverts at the 1,000 m line and back at the seabed. One accent: the red of a visual fault locator, the visible laser an engineer shines into a broken fibre, spent only on the fault, the break and the fault-desk action. Light at the surface and at the seabed, dark in between, because that is where light is. Type contract: one engineered industrial grotesque at display scale with hull-marking proportions and tabular figures (never a reflex face), and one quiet text face whose tabular numerals carry every readout; no monospace identity. Material policy: the cable is the one rendered material (black polyethylene, galvanised steel armour wires, a copper tube, glass fibres in gel); everything else is drawn in hairline section lines like a naval architect's profile (the bow, the sheave, the seabed from bathymetry figures); no glow, no particles, no fog, no caustics. Imagery strategy: code — a procedural 3D armoured cable and grapnel, and drawn SVG sections; no photography, no stock, no generated rasters.
- **STORY:** Chaptered journey; the scroll is the repair operation in its real order. (1) Fault, 0 m — the cable pays out over the bow sheave into the water · the claim that the internet is broken 4,212 m down, the alarm line (first loss of light in UTC, distance from shore), the visitor's own crossing time in milliseconds, and the fault-desk action · the cable enters the water. (2) Located, 0–200 m — the shore station's light pulse runs out along the fibre and its echo returns · three facts: how light finds the break (5 µs per km, the echo at 2,147.3 km), the hours to sail there, why most faults are trawlers and anchors · the haze gives way to blue. (3) Descent, 200–4,212 m — the grapnel goes down through the twilight and the dark, the depth readout running · what is down there, the cable as thick as a garden hose at this depth, how long the wire takes · the seabed arrives in pale ooze. (4) The interruption: the drag — the axis turns: the grapnel crosses the seabed sideways across the cable's line on a held stage while the tension trace runs flat, then jumps when it catches · the catch, in kilonewtons · the lift begins. (5) The splice, on deck — both ends rise and the cable is cut open on screen: sheath, armour, copper, the fibre pairs · the joint, fibre by fibre, with the time it takes · the joint closes. (6) Laid back, and the fault desk — the mended cable goes down again and lies on the seabed in a bight twice the depth long; the light returns with its timestamp · coverage, response time and the fault-desk form · the ship's particulars as the last screen. One interruption (chapter 4); the close is the fault desk and a designed footer; the 404 is the cut end of a fibre.
- **FIRST VIEWPORT:** Archetype: single object with inertia, adapted — the object is the cable on its sheave, held in the right third, never a product turntable. At 1440 × 900: a hairline profile of the Petrel's bow fills the right third from the top edge down to the waterline at about 58 % of the height; the sheave wheel at the bow's tip, and the cable leaving it and running straight down out of the bottom edge, rendered, the only solid object on screen; below the waterline the ground is a shade deeper. Left two thirds: the claim at display scale on three lines, flush left from the 1/12 column, top at about 26 % (built: four balanced lines, "The internet / is broken / 4,212 metres / down.", because the unbreakable "4,212 metres" cannot share a line with "broken" at 88 px; the waterline sits below the claim); under it one line of alarm copy (the time the light stopped in UTC, the distance from shore) and the visitor's own crossing time in milliseconds; then the action "Report a cable fault" as a solid button in the fault red with its ticket number format beside it. The bridge line runs across the top as the navigation: the ship's name and position at the left, the chapter depths as links across, the live depth readout (0 m) and "Fault desk" at the right. Nothing moves on load but the cable, which pays out about 40 cm for 0.9 s (the mechanical register's hero duration) and stops. Phone: the bow and sheave shrink to the top right corner, the cable runs down the right edge, the claim sets on four lines across the full width, the action under it inside the first screen, the bridge line collapses to the name, the depth readout and a chapter menu.
- **SIGNATURE:** The cable pays out. Physical metaphor: a cable engine paying cable over a bow sheave. One continuous armoured cable is held in the right third of the viewport for the whole page, its surface (the helical armour wires) running downward as the visitor scrolls, the grapnel at its end descending through the depth bands, while the bridge line prints the depth, the metres paid out and the line tension. Jobs: (1) it enacts the thesis — the visitor lowers the grapnel to the broken internet with their own scroll; (2) wayfinding — the depth readout is the page's progress and every chapter link is a depth; (3) the chapter transitions — each depth band re-points the theme around the same cable; (4) the explanation — at the seabed the cable turns sideways for the drag, and on deck it opens into its cross-section for the splice. Technique tier: canvas-first, one GL island (a procedural 3D cable and grapnel, lazy) over a DOM document; every number and word is DOM. Touch: the same scroll; coarse pointers get the same readouts. Keyboard: the bridge-line depths are chapter links; the readouts are text. Reduced motion: the cable steps to each chapter's state with no continuous pay-out, the drag and the splice are settled frames that show the hooked cable and the open cable. Fallback without WebGL or on the low tier: a drawn SVG cable with the same helix in a moving stroke pattern, the same readouts.
- **SCROLL MODEL:** Native + Lenis with two sticky stages on invisible rails (the drag, about 180 vh; the splice, about 200 vh). The depth story is a document the reader must be able to scan, jump and return in by depth, so the scroll stays native and find-in-page works; the stages hold only the two moments that need a held frame.
- **LOAD & CLOSE:** No preloader and no gate: the first viewport paints from the DOM with the drawn SVG cable already on its sheave; the GL cable replaces it after its first rendered frame (the real signal), with no shift. Repeat visits change nothing. The last screen is the fault desk (the ticket form, coverage and response time) above the ship's particulars as a designed footer, in the language of a ship's data plate, with the timestamp the light came back. The 404 ships with the first release: the cut end of a fibre, a red point of light, and three routes back.
